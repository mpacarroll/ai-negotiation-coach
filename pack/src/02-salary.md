# Salary and raises

Fourteen scripts. The first five cover a new offer, the next five a raise where you already
work, and the last four the situations that come up in both.

---

## A new offer

### 1. They ask your expectations before you have a number

This is the question that costs people the most money, because answering it first sets the
ceiling and you are guessing in the dark.

> "I would rather hear the range you have budgeted for the role. You know what it is worth to
> you better than I can guess from outside."

If they push a second time, give a range whose **bottom** is a number you would be happy with,
never a range whose top is your target. They will hear the bottom.

> "If it helps, I am looking in the range of [target] to [target plus fifteen percent]."

### 2. They name a number and it is lower than you wanted

Do not accept, do not reject, and do not counter yet. Say almost nothing and let it sit.

> "Thank you for putting a number on it. Can I take a day with that?"

A day is almost always granted, and it converts a live decision you would make under pressure
into a considered one you make at a desk.

### 3. The counter

This is the whole game in four sentences. Number, reason, silence.

> "I am glad we are close. Based on [the specific evidence], I was expecting [your number]. Can
> we get there?"

Then stop. Do not add "but I am flexible." Do not add "if that is not possible I understand."
Both of those hand the number back.

### 4. They say the offer is final

Often true about base pay and almost never true about everything.

> "I understand the base is fixed. Are the other pieces fixed too? A signing amount, the review
> date, the title, the start date, or time off would all matter to me."

A **review date** is the most underrated ask on that list. Moving a review from twelve months to
six is a raise with a delay, and it usually costs the manager nothing today.

### 5. Accepting properly

Get it in writing before you resign from anything.

> "That works. Could you send the written offer with those terms, and I will sign it today?"

## A raise where you already work

### 6. Booking the conversation

Never ambush a manager with this, and never let it ride in as one item on a status meeting.

> "Could we book twenty minutes this week or next? I would like to talk about my compensation.
> I want to give you notice so it is not a surprise."

Telling them the topic in advance is a favor to you, not just to them. A manager blindsided
says no by reflex; a manager who has had three days can arrive with the answer already
half-built.

### 7. The opening

> "I have been here [time] and my scope has changed in a few specific ways: [two or three]. I
> have looked at what the role pays elsewhere and I would like to talk about moving my salary
> to [number]."

Two or three pieces of evidence, not seven. A long list reads as a case being argued, and an
argued case invites a rebuttal. Three strong items land as a statement of fact.

### 8. They say there is no budget

> "I understand this cycle is set. What I am asking for is where this goes next. What would it
> take, and when is the next point where it could actually change?"

Then get a date and a number out of the room. Without both, "no budget" becomes a permanent
condition.

> "So if I land [the specific thing], we revisit at [month] at [number]. Can I write that up
> and send it to you so we are both working from the same note?"

### 9. They say you are already at the top of the band

Bands are real. They are also not the only lever.

> "If I am at the top of the band, then the conversation I should be having is about the next
> band. What does the case for that look like, and who else has to agree?"

This converts a closed question into an open one and gets you the name of the person who
actually decides.

### 10. A promotion arrives with no money attached

> "I am glad you think I am ready for it. Can we talk about the compensation that goes with it?
> I would rather settle that now than take on the scope first and discuss it later."

"I would rather settle that now" is the load-bearing sentence. Scope accepted is leverage
spent.

## Both situations

### 11. They ask what you currently earn

In several US states and cities it is illegal for them to ask. Whether or not it is where you
are, you do not have to answer it.

> "I would rather not anchor this on my last role. What matters is what this role is worth. What
> have you budgeted for it?"

If they insist and you judge that refusing costs you the process, give your **target**, framed
as total compensation, not your current base.

### 12. They ask if you have another offer and you do not

Never invent one. An invented offer is one phone call from ending the conversation, and people
do check.

> "I am not running a process anywhere else. I am asking based on what the work is worth, not on
> a competing number."

Said plainly this is strong, not weak. It says your number has a reason behind it that does not
evaporate when the other offer does.

### 13. Sweeteners worth asking for when cash is stuck

Ask for one or two, not a shopping list. A list reads as someone who will take anything.

- A **review at six months** with a number attached, not a vague revisit
- A **signing amount**, which often comes from a different budget than salary
- **Title**, which costs nothing today and compounds for the rest of your career
- **Time off**, or a fixed remote arrangement in writing
- A **training or conference budget**, the easiest yes on this list
- **Start date** moved back two weeks, which is real money if you are between roles

### 14. Closing, either way

> "Can we make that work?"

Five words, a yes or no question, and then quiet. Most people spoil the close by softening it
into "so, is there any chance we could maybe look at that?" That version is easy to say no to
because it was never really a question.
