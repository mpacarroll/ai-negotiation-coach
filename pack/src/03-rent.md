# Rent

Seven scripts. A landlord's economics are different from an employer's, and the difference is
the whole leverage: **a vacant month costs them more than your increase earns them.** An
apartment empty for one month at $2,400 wipes out a $200 increase for the entire next year.
Most small landlords know this instinctively. Most tenants never say it.

---

### 15. The renewal ask, before they send a number

Timing matters more here than anywhere else in this pack. Ask **sixty to ninety days out**,
before they have set the number, priced the unit, or listed it. After the letter arrives you are
arguing them down from a position they have committed to.

> "I am planning to stay another year. Before you set the renewal, I wanted to ask whether you
> would keep it at the current rate. I pay on time, I have not needed much, and I would rather
> commit now than either of us deal with a turnover."

### 16. The increase letter has already arrived

> "I got the renewal notice. I want to stay, and [new number] is more than I can take on. I can
> commit today at [your number]. Would that work?"

"I can commit today" is the strongest phrase available to a tenant. You are offering certainty,
which is the thing they actually want.

### 17. They will not move on the rate

Then trade for something other than the rate.

> "If the rate is set, could we look at the term instead? I would sign for two years at that
> number if you can hold it flat for the second."

Other things that cost a landlord little and are worth real money to you: the parking space,
a storage unit, a repair or replacement you have been waiting on, the pet fee, or the right to
sublet.

### 18. Naming the vacancy math without being rude

Say it as arithmetic, never as a threat. The moment it sounds like a threat you have invited
them to call it.

> "I know one empty month costs more than the increase brings in over the year. I would rather
> neither of us end up there, which is why I am asking now instead of later."

### 19. You genuinely would move

> "I would like to stay. At [their number] I would be looking at other places, and I would
> rather not spend my autumn doing that. At [your number] I sign today."

Only say this if it is true. If they call it and you stay anyway, you have told them your number
means nothing, and next year's conversation is already lost.

### 20. Signing somewhere new

New leases have more give than renewals, because the unit is empty right now and every day of
that is a loss.

> "I am ready to sign and I can move [their earliest date]. Is there any flexibility on the rent
> for a longer lease, or on the move-in costs?"

Move-in costs are often easier for them to move than rent, because rent sets a number that
shows up in their books every month and a waived fee does not.

### 21. Getting it in writing

> "Could you send the amended terms in writing before I sign? I want to make sure we are both
> working from the same numbers."

A verbal agreement with a landlord is worth exactly as much as the memory of whoever you spoke
to, and property managers change.
