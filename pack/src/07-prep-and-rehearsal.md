# The ten-minute prep

Do this the night before, not in the car. Writing it by hand is better than typing it, because
the point is to have the numbers in your head rather than on a screen you will not open.

**1. Write three numbers.** Anchor, target, walk-away. The anchor sits above the target. The
walk-away is the point where you genuinely stop, and if you cannot name it honestly, say so to
yourself now rather than discovering it mid-sentence.

**2. Write three pieces of evidence.** Three, not seven. Specific, checkable, and about value
delivered rather than effort spent. "I took over the [X] account and it grew forty percent"
beats "I have worked really hard this year."

**3. Write your opening sentence word for word.** Only the opening. Once it is out of your mouth
the conversation is live and a script stops helping, but the first sentence is the one people
fumble.

**4. Say it out loud twice.** Out loud. Reading it silently does not rehearse the part that
actually fails, which is your voice doing something unfamiliar under pressure.

**5. Decide your one concession in advance.** Know the single thing you will trade and what you
want for it. Deciding this live is how people give away the thing they cared about most.

**6. Pick your exit line.** One sentence that ends the meeting without ending the negotiation:
*"Let me think about it and come back to you."* Have it ready so you are never trapped into
deciding.

# Rehearsal prompts

The free tool gives you one prompt for a straightforward practice run. These four are for when
you want it to be harder than the real thing, which is the only way rehearsal is worth the time.

Paste any of these into whichever AI assistant you use.

### The difficult counterpart

> You are the hiring manager for [role]. I am negotiating my offer. Play it realistically and
> make it hard: push back at least three times, use silence, say "that is above our band" at
> least once, and do not concede until I have made a genuine case. My situation: [context]. My
> target: [number]. Start with your first line and stay in character until I say stop.

### The pressure test on your own case

> Here is the case I plan to make for a raise: [paste it]. Do not coach me yet. First, attack
> it. Give me the five strongest objections a skeptical manager would raise, ranked by how much
> damage they do. Then, for each, tell me the shortest honest answer.

### The what-if drill

> I am negotiating [situation]. Run me through six curveballs, one at a time, waiting for my
> answer before the next: they lowball me, they ask what I currently earn, they go silent for a
> week, they give me a deadline of today, they get irritated, they say yes but only verbally.
> After each answer, tell me in one line what a stronger version would have been.

### The afterwards review

> Here is what happened in my negotiation: [what you said, what they said, what you agreed].
> Tell me where I was strong, where I conceded too early, and what specifically I should say
> differently next time. Be blunt. I would rather hear it from you than lose the money again.

## One last thing

Most of the money people leave on the table is not lost to a bad script. It is lost to not
asking at all, or to asking and then filling the silence.

You now have forty-one things to say. The hard part was never the words.
