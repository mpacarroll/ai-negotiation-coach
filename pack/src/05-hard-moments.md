# The hard moments

Nine scripts. This is the section to read the night before. Everything above assumes things go
roughly to plan. These are for when they do not, and they are the reason most people lose money
they had already earned.

---

### 28. You already said a number, and it was too low

The most common disaster, and it is recoverable far more often than people believe. The move is
to reopen on new information, not to contradict yourself.

> "I want to correct something I said earlier. I gave you a number before I had looked properly
> at [the scope, the market, the full picture]. Having done that, the right number is [higher].
> I would rather raise it now than sign something I am not comfortable with."

Do this **as early as possible** and only once. Reopening twice destroys your credibility, and
after that no number you give means anything.

### 29. They go silent for a week

Silence is rarely rejection. It is usually an approval sitting in someone's inbox.

> "Following up on this. Is there anything you need from me to move it along?"

Send it once. Then wait a week and send exactly one more. Three follow-ups in ten days reads as
anxiety, and anxiety is a discount.

### 30. The exploding offer

"We need an answer by end of day" is almost always pressure rather than a real constraint.

> "I want to give you a proper answer rather than a fast one. I can come back to you by [a
> specific, reasonable time]. Does that work?"

Naming your own deadline is what makes this work. "I need more time" invites negotiation about
time. "I will come back Thursday morning" is a plan.

If it genuinely explodes over a two-day extension, you have learned something important about
how they will treat you after you sign.

### 31. You have no walk-away and you know it

Real situations: you need the job, you cannot move, you cannot cancel the service. The instinct
is to bluff. Do not. Bluffs get called and then you have nothing.

> "I want to be straightforward. I am not playing you off against anyone. I am asking because I
> think the number should be [X], and I would like you to consider it."

Asked plainly, with no leverage and no pretence, this works more often than people expect,
because it is disarming and because it is obviously true. What you lose is the ability to
threaten. You keep the ability to ask, and most of the value was in the ask.

### 32. They get personal or irritated

Do not match it and do not apologize for asking.

> "I am not trying to make this difficult. This matters to me, which is why I raised it. Should
> we pick it up another time?"

Offering to pause is not a retreat. It takes the heat out without conceding anything, and the
conversation resumes with the ask still standing.

### 33. You are asked to justify yourself

The trap is over-explaining. Every extra reason is a new thing to attack.

> "I have given you my reasoning. I am comfortable with the number."

Then stop. A number you keep defending starts to sound negotiable. A number you state once
sounds like a fact.

### 34. They counter in the middle and you were not ready

Buy yourself the pause out loud. This is entirely normal and nobody thinks less of you for it.

> "Let me think about that for a moment."

Then actually be quiet for a few seconds. Deciding in the silence is better than deciding while
talking, and talking while deciding is how people accept things they did not mean to.

### 35. You get an emotional yes and want to lock it in

The riskiest moment in the whole process, because it feels finished and it is not.

> "That is great, thank you. Could you put that in an email so I have it?"

Verbal agreements evaporate, budgets get revisited, and the person who said yes may not be the
person who processes it. Ask warmly and ask immediately.

### 36. Deciding whether to accept, afterwards

Not a script, a test. Ask yourself one question:

**If this exact deal is still the deal in twelve months, and nothing has improved, am I fine
with that?**

If yes, take it. If no, what you actually need is not a better number today but a written date
when it changes, which is script 8.
